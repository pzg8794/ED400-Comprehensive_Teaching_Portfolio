# Proficiency 5 — Assessment of Learning

## Claim

I understand assessment as an ongoing process of gathering evidence about student learning in order to guide instruction, support growth, and make achievement visible through multiple forms of demonstration.

## Assessment as Evidence, Not Sorting

My learning in this area has been shaped by both coursework and unit planning. Assessment is most useful when it helps a teacher understand what students know, where they are getting stuck, and what supports or extensions are needed next. This stance is especially important in inclusive and technology-rich classrooms, where students may show understanding in ways that differ from traditional written responses.

In my ED452B innovative unit plan, assessment was embedded across the unit rather than treated only as an endpoint. Students had opportunities to demonstrate planning, collaboration, application, and reflection throughout the sequence. This kind of distributed assessment better captures learning in project-based computer science environments, where understanding often appears in the process of building, revising, explaining, and troubleshooting.

## Multiple Measures and Accessibility

My approach to assessment is also shaped by disability-conscious and equity-centered thinking. If assessments rely too heavily on one format, one language demand, or one pace of completion, they may reveal less about learning than about barriers. For that reason, I want to use a range of formative and summative measures, including observation, discussion, project artifacts, self-reflection, and performance tasks. The goal is not to lower rigor, but to increase validity by ensuring students have more than one way to show what they know.

My background in data science additionally influences how I think about assessment. Evidence matters, but evidence must be interpreted carefully. Numbers alone never tell the whole story. A strong assessment practice requires context, teacher judgment, and awareness of what the measure may be missing.

## Reflection

I am still developing this proficiency, especially in designing strong rubrics and translating observational evidence into clear next instructional steps. What has changed most is that I now see assessment less as grading and more as instructional listening. Good assessment helps the teacher hear what the student is already showing.
