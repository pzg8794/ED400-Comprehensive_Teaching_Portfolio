# Proficiency 6 — Planning for Instruction

## Claim

I plan instruction intentionally by aligning objectives, materials, scaffolds, assessments, and participation structures so that learning builds coherently over time and remains accessible to diverse learners.

## Planning as Architecture

Planning for instruction has become one of the clearest places where I can see the difference between a strong idea and a teachable lesson. A meaningful lesson or unit does not emerge from content alone; it emerges from how the content is sequenced, represented, practiced, and revisited. My ED452B innovative unit plan and EDE498C materials gave me repeated practice in thinking architecturally: identifying outcomes, breaking them into manageable stages, anticipating barriers, and designing scaffolds that preserve rigor while widening access.

The Minecraft unit plan especially deepened this skill. Planning the unit required me to consider how students would be introduced to tools, how much novelty they could absorb at once, where modeling was necessary, what independent work should look like, and how students would move from guided participation to greater ownership. Revision across the multi-part portfolio made visible that planning is iterative. Effective plans improve when feedback reveals where transitions, directions, or supports are still too thin.

## Planning for Inclusion and Flexibility

Instructional planning also means planning for the variability of real classrooms. Students do not all begin from the same starting point, and they do not all respond to the same representation, timing, or structure. Because of this, I want my planning to remain flexible. I try to build multiple entry points, clear routines, and alternative pathways into the task so that a lesson is not dependent on one narrow form of engagement.

In computer science and technology-centered lessons, planning must also account for tool issues, troubleshooting time, and the emotional impact of not knowing what to do next. Good planning helps prevent frustration from taking over the lesson.

## Reflection

I have grown in this area by learning to anticipate more. Earlier in my program, I was more likely to focus on what I wanted students to do; now I spend more time considering what students will need in order to do it. That shift has made my planning more realistic, more inclusive, and more instructionally coherent.
