# Proficiency 2 — Learner Diversity and Equity

## Claim

I understand learner diversity and equity as central to teaching rather than supplementary to it, and I design instruction with explicit attention to disability, neurodivergence, language difference, cultural identity, access, and the institutional patterns that often position these differences as deficits.

## Equity as Interpretive Work

My strongest evidence for this proficiency comes from my EDU442 work, particularly the Deconstructing Bias paper and the Impacting Practice AI project. In both projects, I examined how schools produce inequity not only through policy, but through everyday interpretations of students. In the Bias Deconstruct paper, I traced how neurodivergent and culturally and linguistically diverse students can quickly become categorized as “problem students” when adults read visible frustration, noncompliance, or nonstandard communication as character failure rather than contextual information. That paper pushed me to confront my own bias and develop a more careful practice of reading behavior as communication.

The AI professional development module extended that equity lens into computer science and educational technology. The project argued that AI use in schools is not socially neutral: without explicit structures, AI tools can deepen inequity by rewarding students who already know how to navigate technology, process language-heavy prompts, and manage authorship expectations. In response, I created a teacher-facing module that helps educators use AI in ways that protect student voice, privacy, agency, and access.

## Diversity in Practice

My commitment to diversity and equity is also grounded in my own identity and academic pathway. As a Dominican-American, first-generation, neurodivergent scholar working across data science and education, I know that institutions often misrecognize difference before they know how to support it. That autobiographical understanding strengthens my commitment to culturally sustaining and disability-conscious pedagogy. I want my classroom to be one where students do not have to perform narrow versions of competence in order to be treated as intelligent.

In practice, this means designing for multiple forms of participation, building supports into lessons from the beginning, and staying alert to how language, timing, tool choice, and classroom norms privilege some students over others. It also means treating equity as ongoing professional work rather than a single statement of belief.

## Reflection

This proficiency has become one of the anchors of my identity as an educator. I no longer see diversity as a list of categories to acknowledge; I see it as a reality that requires teachers to rethink curriculum, communication, participation, and interpretation. My goal is to build classrooms where equity is visible not only in what I say, but in how I plan, respond, assess, and advocate.
